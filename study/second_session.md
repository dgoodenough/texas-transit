# The second session: the cabinet and the metros review the whole plan

*November 2026. The governor, the five cabinet members from the first session (`study/hsr_buildout.md`, `study/cabinet/`)
and the transit planners of the big metros review the plan as it now stands: high-speed rail redrawn and costed by
context, the Connectivity Program, the trunk-first gate, and every metro network redrawn by the line planner. The
people are personas. Every number they cite comes from the repository's own runs (`study/hsr_buildout.md`, "Where the
plan stands"; `study/urban_metros.md`; `study/router.md`; `docs/data/timeline.json`), unless it's marked as outside
knowledge.*

## The ground rules

**Governor Marisol Vega**: "Last time the cabinet was polite to the model and rude to each other. This time I want it
the other way round. Nobody leaves without filing three things:

1. **The fight**: the thing in this plan you'd oppose in public.
2. **The big swing**: the most aggressive move you'd make if I said yes.
3. **The number you don't believe**: one figure the model produces that you'd bet against.

Every item gets a written answer from me: adopt, test, or reject with a reason. Nothing gets dropped because the person
who raised it was outnumbered. And the best-argued dissent in this room gets the first model run of the next round."

## Where the plan stands (what everyone read)

- **Budget**: $9.0B a year statewide to 2100 ($2.3B of it DFW's own plan), about $682B in all.
- **Best ratio first** (one queue): every one of the 80 metro lines is built by 2100. New daily metro trips reach 1.60M in
  2050 and 4.89M in 2100. 15 of 16 high-speed corridors and 6 of the 7 branches open, but not West Texas. Net benefit
  in 2100 is $22.0B a year.
- **The whole map** (the Connectivity Program on its own money): all 7 branches open by 2085. Everything is built by
  2084, after which the plan spends nothing: **$497B spent, about $185B never used**.
- **The metros**, after the line planner (`study/router.md`):

| Metro | Lines | Capital | Its budget share | Daily trips (today's land use) | 2100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Houston | 26 | $60.3B | $165.1B | 369,231 | 1.40M |
| San Antonio | 17 | $24.1B | $59.1B | 158,563 | 0.54M |
| Austin | 18 | $35.3B | $52.7B | 249,407 | 1.36M |
| El Paso | 6 | $8.3B | $20.1B | 49,758 | 0.10M |
| McAllen | 2 | $2.0B | $8.0B | 8,630 | 0.03M |
| Brownsville-Harlingen | 2 | $0.7B | $3.9B | 3,465 | 0.01M |
| DFW (its own plan) | 75 | $135B | $2.3B a year | | 1.72M |

- **The gap no budget pays**: running everything costs $4.7-5.0B a year more than fares bring in by 2100.
- **The Auditor's gate**: no corridor in the plan, the Triangle included, clears a ratio of 0.8 at +45% cost and -30% riders.

## The cabinet

### State Auditor

**The fight: the metro floor is circular.** The line planner keeps a change to a metro network only if it carries 2.5
daily trips per $M, which was chosen because that's a ratio of about 0.37, where the statewide queue runs out of money.
But the queue runs out where it does *because* of what the metros spend. Cut the metros, money frees up, and the queue
reaches further down: the marginal ratio falls below 0.37. The floor and the queue have to be solved together, to a fixed
point, not set once from a run that assumed the old networks. Until then, 2.5 is a number we picked, not one we found.

**The big swing: make the decade review bite.** Today it prints "a policy choice" next to 16 of 23 corridors and nothing
happens. Tie it to money: a corridor that fails the stress gate and isn't designated gets protected right-of-way and
design work, not construction, until it passes or the Legislature designates it by name. Then the designation means
something.

**The number I don't believe: "every metro line is built by 2100."** It's true only because the planner first cut the
networks from 166 lines to 80. The model's line values are judged in 2050 on one fixed land use, with no crowding, so more frequency
always helps on paper. The capacity check runs on the old sketch's peak load, not the full model's. A 4-minute metro
headway looks free of crowding because the model can't see crowding.

### Secretary of Finance

**The fight: the operating gap.** We plan $9B a year of capital and then run up a $4.7-5.0B a year operating gap by 2100
that no line in this plan pays. That's more than half the capital budget again, every year, forever. I won't sign a plan
that treats operations as somebody else's problem.

**The big swing: turn the unspent $185B into an operating endowment.** The whole map finishes building in 2084 and then
sits on about $185B. Bank the money the plan doesn't need as it goes: an endowment that size, drawn at 3-4% real, pays
$5.5-7.4B a year, which covers the whole gap. That's the difference between a network that's built and one that runs.

**The number I don't believe: fares.** The model's fares cover 16% of the cost of running the metro lines ($0.6B of
$4.0B a year), and it never asks whether a fare policy changes anything. A VMT fee on driving, which the first session recommended, would
raise money *and* riders; the plan counts neither.

**Against the "spend it all" camp** (see below): at the generator's own floor of 1 trip per $M, the metros would spend
$113B more and carry 0.43M more daily trips in 2100 (5.31M against 4.89M, the whole map). The net benefit in 2100 is
flat ($21.5B against $21.6B), and the operating gap grows by $1.6B a year. Every extra rider costs more to carry than
they're worth to the plan.

### Secretary of Infrastructure

**The fight: boom and bust.** Under the whole map the state spends $254B by 2050, finishes in 2084 and then stops. My
learning curve (10% cheaper per doubling of kilometres built) assumes a delivery office that never goes idle. Stop for
16 years and you lose the crews, the suppliers and the institutional memory; the next project costs what the first one
did. The schedule also lets every corridor and metro line be under construction at once. Nobody has the workforce for
that; Spain, our cost benchmark, built its network in a steady stream.

**The big swing: a steady pipeline, paid for by the unspent money.** Spread construction so the office is never idle:
upgrade the higher-speed legs (Panhandle, West Texas) to full speed as soon as they open, not when they reach 3M trips;
double-track the single-track legs; build Houston-San Antonio direct; offer Mexico the Laredo-Monterrey track. The same
crews, the same standard designs, all the way to 2100.

**The number I don't believe: the metro tunnel rule.** Every metro line is put in a tunnel within the downtown core.
Austin's two big metro lines cost $8.7B and $9.2B, in good part because of that. Elevated or at-grade in a dedicated right-of-way
is a third of the cost per kilometre where the street allows it. The generator never asks.

### Secretary of Housing and Land Use

**The fight: budgets by 2020 heads.** Each metro's share is DFW's rate per *2020* resident, spread over the horizon.
Austin grows from 2.28M people to 8.66M by 2100 and gets $0.7B a year, the stream of a 2.3M-person city. Under metros
first, Austin finishes half its network by 2061 and the last line in 2082. San Antonio and Houston look roomy only because
they grow more slowly.

**The big swing: station districts by right, statewide.** Only 17% of Houston's people live within 800 m of a station on
its new network (Austin 23%). The model's land-use package steers a fifth of growth to station areas; make it law instead
of an assumption: mid-rise by right and no parking minimums within 800 m of every station the plan builds, in every city.

**The number I don't believe: Austin at 10.9%.** Austin's transit share reaches 10.9% in 2100 with this network, against
6.7% for Houston. Maybe; Austin is young and dense near UT. But the difference is mostly land use the model assumes will
happen, and that's exactly the part no one has legislated.

### Chief Network Planner

**The fight: through-running.** The planner tried every merge the network offered and kept almost none: Houston runs 1
of its 7 metro lines through downtown. That's the model's arithmetic, not a finding about Houston. The model charges
nothing for a downtown terminal: no tail tracks, no layover space, no stub-end platforms under expensive land. A
through line costs the same downtown as two stubs, so it only wins on the 5-minute transfer penalty. Put the terminal
costs in and through-running will come back.

**The big swing: the airports.** Hobby, San Antonio International and El Paso International have no station, because
their terminals sit off the road graph the routes snap to. Hand-draw each airport connector as a short spur (the
generator can take a fixed alignment), and score it on the model. And finish the router: turn penalties are still not
done, and the local search could be replaced by the exact integer program for the smaller metros, where it's tractable.

**The number I don't believe: San Antonio's airport line.** Its best line is labeled "San Antonio Airport - Flores /
Southcross", carrying 23,763 trips a day. But the center it calls "San Antonio Airport" is 2.2 km from the terminal:
it's an airport line that stops short of the airport.

## The metros

### Houston (METRO)

**The fight: two lines by 2040.** Under best ratio first, Houston opens 2 of its 26 lines by 2040, half by 2057 and the
last in 2071, while the state builds I-10 high-speed rail to San Antonio in 2033 and Texas Central in 2035. Under metros
first, it's 13 by 2040 and half by 2042. A region of 7 million people, 11 million by 2050, gets two lines in fifteen
years so that intercity trains run first. Houston's own share is $165B; the plan spends $60B of it.

**The big swing: let Houston spend its share.** At the generator's own floor, Houston's network was 55 lines for $105.8B
and 450,728 daily trips (today's land use); at the plan's floor it's 26 lines, $60.3B and 369,231. Give the metros their
own streams (metros first), let each spend its full share at the generator's floor, and let the statewide queue have
only what a metro can't use. Houston's own riders, not the statewide queue's arithmetic, should decide Houston's network.

**The number I don't believe: one through line.** One of seven metro lines through downtown is not what any Houston
planner would draw. See the Chief Network Planner's terminal-cost point; we agree.

### Dallas-Fort Worth (DFW Transit's plan)

**The fight: Fort Worth-Dallas in 2064.** Under best ratio first, the high-speed link between the two halves of the
state's biggest region opens in 2064, the same year as Shreveport. The model values it on top of the TRE and DFW Transit's own
regional rail, so it adds little, and that's fair as far as it goes. But it's the link that makes Fort Worth a stop on
every Texas Central and I-35 train instead of a transfer. The model doesn't count through trains from Fort Worth.

**The big swing: DFW's plan as the template.** DFW's plan is hand-drawn: lines through downtown Dallas and Fort Worth,
branches as service patterns, 1.72M daily trips in 2100 for $135B. Use it to calibrate the generator: run the line
planner on DFW and see how close it gets to the hand-drawn plan. If it can't reproduce DFW, it shouldn't be drawing
Houston.

**The number I don't believe: a fixed $2.3B a year.** DFW's stream is fixed while the other metros now leave money on
the table. The plan should let DFW bid for that money on the same terms as everyone else.

### San Antonio (VIA)

**The fight: half our share unspent.** San Antonio builds $24.1B of a $59.1B share. The line planner cut 22 lines from
the old network and kept 17. Under best ratio first, half open by 2049.

**The big swing: the model found our Green Line, so build it first.** The generator's best line, 23,763 trips a day, runs
the same north-south corridor through downtown that VIA's Green Line is already building (outside knowledge). Build it
and its east-west partner by 2035, with a real terminal at the airport.

**The number I don't believe: 84% to 49%.** The overlap of our BRT lines fell from 84% to 49%. The model counts two BRT
lines sharing a busway downtown as duplication; we'd call it a trunk.

### Austin (CapMetro, Austin Transit Partnership)

**The fight: our stream.** See the Housing Secretary: $0.7B a year for a region that quadruples. Our network is
the best per dollar of the big four (6,213 trips per $B, all four anchors served, UT included) and the slowest to finish
under metros first.

**The big swing: let growth pay.** Austin's land values near stations will rise faster than anywhere in Texas; the land-tax
bonds the model already counts pay only 9% of urban capital statewide. Let Austin borrow against its own growth, a
tax-increment district on every station area, and finish by 2050.

**The number I don't believe: the metro tunnels.** Same as Infrastructure: $8.7B and $9.2B for our two metro lines
assumes tunnels downtown. Our own light rail plan runs at grade on Guadalupe and Trinity (outside knowledge).

### El Paso (Sun Metro)

**The fight: the border doesn't exist in this model.** El Paso has 0.87M people. Ciudad Juárez, across the river, has
about 1.5M (outside knowledge), and the bridges carry more people every day than most of our lines would. The model counts
none of them. Our network is 6 lines, $8.3B of a $20.1B share, 49,758 daily trips; West Texas high-speed rail never
arrives under best ratio first and arrives in 2069 under the whole map.

**The big swing: a cross-border line.** A line from downtown El Paso across a bridge to Juárez's center, built with
Mexico, and the border factor the first session used for Laredo and the Valley applied to El Paso, Juárez included.
And the airport: it's 10 minutes from downtown and the network doesn't reach it.

**The number I don't believe: 0 of 1 through downtown.** Our only metro line ends downtown. Downtown El Paso sits on the
border. A through line from the east side would stop at the bridge, and the model can't see what's on the other side.

### The Rio Grande Valley (McAllen, Brownsville-Harlingen)

**The fight: 2071.** Under best ratio first, Brownsville-Harlingen's first metro line opens in 2071. McAllen opens one
in 2037 and the other in 2073. Under metros first, both are open by 2032. The Valley's high-speed line has the best
ratio of the seven branches (1.33) and still waits until 2090 under best ratio first, 2074 under the whole map, sixth of the seven.

**The big swing: the Valley first, not last.** The branch with the best ratio should open first among the branches, not
sixth. Start the Gulf spine from Corpus Christi as higher-speed rail in the 2040s, and give the Valley's three cities one
regional network: McAllen, Harlingen and Brownsville are a single 1.3M-person region the model splits in two.

**The number I don't believe: the constants.** McAllen's ridership constant is -0.14 and Brownsville's +0.18, fitted to
almost no transit today. A region with no service gets a constant that says nobody wants service. That's circular.

### The mid-size cities (Corpus Christi, Lubbock, Laredo, Amarillo and the rest)

**The fight: one line each, and zero for some.** Corpus Christi, Lubbock, Laredo and Amarillo get one BRT line each.
Killeen, Beaumont, Longview, Tyler, Abilene, Midland, Wichita Falls, Sherman, San Angelo, Victoria,
Texarkana and Eagle Pass get none. Laredo's one line opens in
2052 under best ratio first. These are the cities the high-speed branches are for, and the branches arrive at a city with
no transit at the station.

**The big swing: a station network.** Every city a high-speed line serves gets a guaranteed BRT or frequent-bus line from
its station to its downtown and its biggest campus or base (Fort Cavazos, Texas Tech), paid from the Connectivity Program,
opening with the station.

**The number I don't believe: zero for Midland.** The densest corridor in the Permian doesn't reach the generator's BRT
threshold, so Midland gets nothing, and the West Texas line will stop there.

## The floor

The arguments that took the room.

**1. "Leave the money on the table?"** Houston, San Antonio and the Valley wanted their full shares. Finance answered with
the floor comparison: $113B more for 0.43M more daily trips in 2100, no gain in net benefit, $1.6B a year more operating
gap. The Auditor sided with neither: the floor is circular (above), and nobody can say what the right one is until it's
solved together with the queue. **Houston's reply** carried the room on one point: the problem isn't the total, it's the
*order*. Two Houston lines by 2040 is a scheduling result, not a budget one; metros first gets 13.

**2. "Who owns the unspent $185B?"** Finance (an operating endowment), Infrastructure (a steady pipeline), Houston and
DFW (more metro lines) all claimed it. Finance's case is that it's the only proposal that fixes something nobody else
pays for. Infrastructure's is that the pipeline is what makes the costs real. They agreed the two fit: an endowment funded
while the office stays busy on upgrades that pay for themselves in operating savings.

**3. "Is through-running a model blind spot?"** Unanimous among the planners, and the Chief Network Planner's diagnosis
(no terminal costs downtown) is testable. The Auditor's condition: test it with costs from real terminal projects, not a
number chosen to produce through lines.

**4. "Should the best branch wait?"** The Valley's planner pointed out that the Connectivity Program orders branches by
the trunk-first gate, not by ratio, so the Valley (1.33) waits for Corpus Christi's trunk. The Network Planner: the Gulf
spine trunk to Corpus Christi is itself low in the queue; build it as higher-speed first, earlier, and the Valley follows.

**5. "The border."** El Paso, Laredo and the Valley together: the model ignores 3-4 million people across the river.
Land Use reminded the room that the first session's border factors were only applied to the high-speed branches, never to
the metros. Nobody disagreed.

**6. "Model the city before you build it."** DFW's challenge (run the planner on DFW and compare it with the hand-drawn
plan) became the room's favorite test: it's the one place in Texas where we know what good looks like.

## The plan of work

In the order the governor set:

1. **The fixed point** (item 1): iterate the metro floor with the queue. Every other test runs on its result.
2. **What the model can't see** (items 3, 10, 11, 18, 22): crowding, terminal costs, airport spurs, border demand in the
   metros, and constants where there's no service today.
3. **The money** (items 4, 5, 6, 8, 13, 16): operations as a budget line, the endowment against the pipeline, growth-based
   metro streams, a minimum stream per metro in one queue.
4. **The network** (items 7, 17, 20, 21, 23): cheaper metro grades, Fort Worth through trains, the Gulf spine earlier,
   one Valley network, the station network.
5. **The acceptance test** (item 15): the line planner on DFW against the hand-drawn plan.

Items 2, 9 and 23 are policy, adopted now; the model reports them but doesn't wait for them.
