# Timing: one statewide plan under one budget

Generated by `python study/timing.py`. Every project from steps 3-5 and Dallas-Fort Worth's own plan, valued the same way and scheduled under the statewide budget: $9.0B a year of capital (DFW Transit's rate per resident), 2025-2100, in 2025 dollars.

**Dallas-Fort Worth** runs DFW Transit's 2 million riders plan on its own timetable (`data/dfw/dfw_plan.json`). It spends $135.0B of its $174.8B, and every year the rest of the state gets what DFW doesn't spend, plus the land-tax bonds DFW's new stations raise: $556.5B in all for everything else.

**Value.** Travellers' gain, from the same logit models that predict the riders (the logsum gain of every intercity traveller on a pair; for the urban commute model one money coefficient's worth per new trip, $8), plus wider benefits (`texas/benefits.json`): congestion, crashes and emissions avoided for each car-km off the road ($0.25 a km in the metros, $0.11 between them) and air passenger-km ($0.03), and agglomeration (20% of travellers' gain for urban lines, 10% for high-speed rail). An urban trip is worth about $12 in all. The **ratio** is benefit over cost across the 50 years from a project's opening at 3%, with travel growing as forecast (Texas's population for intercity lines, each metro's own 2026, 2050 and 2100 model runs for urban ones): all benefits (plus any operating surplus) over capital plus the operating deficit. Projects open best ratio first as the budget allows (spending spread over the construction years, carryover allowed, no borrowing beyond the land-tax bonds); the budget is spent whether or not a ratio reaches 1.

**Land tax.** Each new urban line's new stations form a Tax Increment Reinvestment Zone and a Public Improvement District (800 m around them): the city and county tax on the rise in value the line brings, plus a 0.1% assessment, pledged for 30 years and borrowed against when the line opens. The bonds pay part of the line's capital, so the statewide budget pays less; they don't change its ratio (the rise in land value is travellers' gain showing up in prices).

## Caveats

- Wider benefits are per-km and per-trip allowances from the ranges in US and UK practice (`texas/benefits.json`), not modelled here: no traffic model says where the cars come off the road, and agglomeration is a share of travellers' gain. Halve them and most urban lines fall back under 1.
- The urban figure for travellers' gain is an approximation (exact only as transit's share goes to zero) and counts non-commute trips at the commute value; the intercity figure leaves out induced trips' own gain. Both include each mode's constant, so the ratios are only as good as those constants (high-speed rail's moves its first four corridors between 30M and 74M trips a year).
- Land-tax revenue assumes the uplift arrives with the line and the districts are formed: Texas cities form TIRZs and PIDs one at a time, and school taxes, the biggest share, can't be captured. Values are 2020 people and jobs at today's prices, with no growth near the stations (conservative).
- Intercity values grow with Texas's population trend from 2050. Urban values follow each metro's model runs in 2026, 2050 and 2100 (study/urban_metros.md), which open every line from 2026: a line opened late is credited with station areas that have had decades to develop, so late lines' riders are on the high side. DFW's numbers are its own model's, on its own growth path.
- An urban line's riders are its share of its metro's total, by the full model's value of it (the mean of what the network loses without it and what it adds alone). Lines that work as a network share the credit, so fewer of them reach a ratio of 1 on their own than when the sketch's single-line trips split the total.
- Operating cost is outside the capital budget, as in DFW Transit; the deficits are in the ratio, but no budget pays them yet.
