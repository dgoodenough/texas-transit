# The third session: the cabinet reviews the final plan

*November 2026. The governor and the five cabinet members from the first two sessions review the plan as it stands after
the second session's 26 items were tested and the networks regenerated (`study/second_session.md`, "What came of it").
The people are personas. Every number they cite comes from the repository's own runs (`study/timing.md`,
`study/session2_tests.md`, `study/fixed_point.md`, `study/cross_border.md`, `study/urban_metros.md`,
DFW Transit's `reports/generator_dfw_check.md`), unless it's marked as outside knowledge.*

## The ground rules

**Governor Marisol Vega**: "Same three things from each of you: the fight, the big swing, the number you don't believe.
Two changes. First, the model has now answered most of what you asked it last time, so I don't want to hear a question
the repository already answers; I want to hear what you'd *do* with the answers. Second, this is the version we take
to the Legislature. If something in it would embarrass us in a hearing, say it here."

## Where the plan stands (what everyone read)

- **Budget**: $9.0B a year statewide to 2100 ($2.3B of it DFW's own plan), about $682B in all.
- **Present value of net benefits, 2025-2100 at 3%**: **$192.5B** best ratio first, $167.3B metros first, **$218.0B**
  the whole map.
- **By 2100** (best ratio first): 4.89M new daily metro trips (1.72M in DFW), 106M intercity rail trips a year, about
  325 fewer flights a day, $18.7B a year of travellers' gain and $8.7B of wider benefits, and **$4.5B a year more to
  operate than fares bring in**.
- **What clears the bar**: 19 of the 80 metro lines and 2 of the 16 high-speed lines (Texas Central 1.06, I-35 south
  1.00) have a ratio of 1 or more. The median metro line is 0.75. Under the Auditor's stress (cost x1.45, riders x0.70)
  **2 of 80 metro lines clear 0.8 and no high-speed line does** (Texas Central falls to 0.44).
- **The order**: a search over the build order raises the plan's value by **4.0%** ($191.1B to $198.8B in its own
  accounting). The plan still builds best ratio first.
- **The whole map** spends $477.7B by 2085 and then stops: about **$204B never spent**. Banked from 2085 it pays 117% of
  2100's operating gap for good.

- **The second session's tests**: guaranteed metro streams −$10.4B; an 800 km construction cap −$6.3B; the Gulf spine

## The cabinet

### State Auditor

**The fight: we're about to ask for $682B on a stress gate nothing passes.** I asked for the gate in the first session
and the plan has reported it faithfully ever since. Now read it. No high-speed corridor clears 0.8 at +45% cost and
−30% riders, Texas Central included, and 78 of 80 metro lines don't either. Those aren't exotic assumptions: they're the
average overrun and the average ridership miss in the literature the model cites. A hearing will read "fails the
stress gate" next to every line in the plan, and we'll have no answer except "we built them anyway." The decade review
already gives undesignated corridors right-of-way and design only. That isn't enough when the designated ones fail too.

**The big swing: no construction contract until the corridor's own numbers pass.** Make the gate binding at the point
of commitment, not the point of planning: every corridor and every metro line keeps its place in the plan, gets its
design and right-of-way, and signs construction only when its refreshed cost estimate (at 30% design) and refreshed
ridership forecast clear 0.8 under the stress. The money for one that fails waits in the fund for the next decade's
review. That turns the stress gate from a column in a table into the plan's brake, and it's the only answer I'd accept
for a hearing.

**The number I don't believe: $8.7B a year of wider benefits.** They're per-km allowances and a 20% agglomeration
markup, not anything the model computes, and they're a third of the plan's benefits. Halve them and most urban lines fall
back under 1 (the plan's own caveat). I'd publish the plan's present value with them halved, next to the headline.

**And one more: take the 4%.** The build-order search finds $7.7B more value for the same money and the same projects.
We print it and don't use it. Either adopt the optimized order or say why not.

### Secretary of Finance

**The fight: $4.5B a year that nobody has agreed to pay.** By 2100 the network costs $4.5B a year more to run than its
fares bring in, and under best ratio first nothing in the plan pays it. The endowment works only in the whole map, and
only from 2085, when building stops. Before that, every new line adds to a deficit some city or transit authority has to
fund out of its own budget. METRO, VIA and CapMetro will each be asked to run lines they didn't choose, with money they
don't have. That's where this plan dies: not in the Legislature, in the city councils.

**The big swing: bank the money early, and adopt the one money test that pays.** Two moves. First, doubling the
station-area bonds where a metro outgrows Texas (Austin, Houston, San Antonio, Odessa) added $2.4B of value and spent
$10.6B less. Adopt it now. Second, start the operating endowment in 2050, not 2085: put a fixed share of each year's
budget into it from then on, so the deficit is funded as it grows. It will cost some construction. I want to know how
much value that costs and how much deficit it covers.

**The number I don't believe: land-tax bonds pay 8% of the metro lines' capital.** That assumes every city forms a TIRZ
and a PID around every new station, the uplift arrives with the line, and the bonds sell at the model's rate. Texas
cities form these one at a time, and school taxes, the biggest share, can't be captured. I'd plan on half.

### Secretary of Infrastructure

**The fight: the whole map can't be built at the pace it assumes.** At its peak it has 1,686 km of high-speed track
under construction at once. No country has done that. China came closest, with a central ministry and a decade of
practice behind it (outside knowledge). Capping it at 800 km costs $6.3B of value and pushes the last branch from 2085 to
2091. I say that's not a cost, it's the price of a real plan. The uncapped version is a schedule nobody could staff.

**The big swing: a Texas rail delivery office with a fixed pipeline, and credit for learning only with it.** The whole
map's high-speed costs fall as "a delivery office that learns": unit costs drop with each project. That learning only
happens if the same people build one project after another without a gap. So tie them: the whole map runs at the 800 km
cap, and the learning-curve savings are counted only under the steady pipeline. If the steady office earns back more
than the $6.3B the cap costs, the decision makes itself.

**The number I don't believe: the high-speed unit costs once the office learns.** The whole map's costs assume we
deliver at a fraction of California's. The P10-P90 ranges help, but I'd publish the whole map at today's costs beside it,
not only in the levers panel.

### Secretary of Housing and Land Use

**The fight: the metros' 2100 riders rest on zoning we haven't passed.** Every metro's 2050 and 2100 riders assume DFW's
station-area responses: more homes and jobs near stations, a fifth of growth steered there. Austin's counties reach 3.8
times their 2020 population on a trend extrapolation, and every late line is credited with station areas that have had
decades to grow (the plan's own caveat). Station districts by right is our adopted policy, but nothing ties the money to
it. A city that keeps single-family zoning around a new station still gets the line, and the plan still counts the riders.

**The big swing: no zoning, no line.** A metro line's construction contract waits until its city has zoned the station
district by right. Pair it with the Auditor's gate: the refreshed forecast at 30% design uses the zoning the city has
actually adopted. Cities that move fast get their lines first, and the forecasts stop assuming what hasn't happened.

**The number I don't believe: Austin at 3.8 times its 2020 population.** I'd test the plan with every metro growing at
Texas's rate, to see which lines depend on growth that may not come.

### Chief Network Planner

**The fight: the networks are good, but nobody outside this room can read them.** The generator passed the one test
where we know what good looks like: on DFW, given the hand plan's money, it carries 5% more riders. But look at what we
publish. "Pellicano / George Dieter - Nonap / Mark Twain BRT" is a line. Four of El Paso's six lines read like that, and
three lines across the state have no name at all. 936 of 1,147 stations are named by the nearest cross streets. DFW's
site has named, colored lines, a trip planner, station pages and a schematic map. Ours has a cross-section of tables. A
resident of Houston can't find out what the plan means for them, and a legislator can't name a single line in their
district. That's the hearing problem.

**The big swing: every metro gets DFW's site, and the state gets a trip planner across all of it.** Named lines in a color
system each metro can keep (Houston's existing Red, Green and Purple first), stations named by landmark and
neighbourhood, a trip planner that runs from a Houston bus stop to an Austin station on high-speed rail, and a schematic
map per metro. Then hand each metro's planners the tables to edit, as DFW's were. The generator becomes the check, not
the author. The governor asked for the review of what that takes; it's in `study/site_parity.md`.

**The number I don't believe: San Antonio's S416 at 1,000 an hour.** It's over capacity even at a bus every 6 minutes
in 2050. A corridor that full in the model's middle case is a rail corridor, and the plan still draws it as BRT. Test it
as rail.

## The floor

**1. "Brake or plan?"** The Auditor's binding gate drew the sharpest exchange. The Network Planner: "If nothing passes,
the gate isn't a brake, it's a stop." The Auditor: "Then the plan should say what it costs to get one line through it, and
build that one first." Finance sided with the Auditor on construction contracts and with the planner on design work:
keep designing everything, commit only what passes.

**2. "Why not take the 4%?"** Nobody defended best ratio first on value. The Network Planner's one objection: the
optimized order moves some Houston lines from the 2060s to the 2090s, and metros will fight any order that pushes them
back. The room's answer was to adopt the optimized order and report each metro's first line under both.

**3. "Cap or no cap?"** Infrastructure and Finance split. Finance: the cap costs $6.3B for something the model doesn't
value. Infrastructure: the model doesn't value it because it assumes an office that can't exist. They agreed on the test:
count the learning only under the cap.

**4. "Whose site is it?"** Land Use and the Network Planner together: the public face of the plan is what the Legislature
will judge, and today it serves DFW well and the rest of the state poorly. The governor asked for the parity review the
same day.

## The plan of work

1. **What the Legislature will ask** (items 1, 2, 3, 6, 11): the binding gate's report, the plan with wider benefits
   halved, the optimized order, half the land tax, and state-rate growth. One run each on the current networks.
2. **The money** (items 4, 5, 7): the doubled bonds into the plan, the 2050 endowment, the capped and learning whole map.
3. **The network** (items 9, 13): the Gulf spine early into the whole map, and S416 as rail.
4. **The public face** (items 8, 12): the parity review, then the site in its phases.

Items 1, 4, 9 and 10 are adopted now; the plan reports them, and the tests above don't wait for them.
