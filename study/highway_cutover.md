# Highway cutover: when is a transit dollar worth less than a dollar on widening?

Generated by `python study/highway_cutover.py`. **Directional only.** One representative widened Texas urban freeway, valued with the plan's own framework (`study/timing.py`: 50 years at 3%, the plan's value of time, the same car externalities and agglomeration share), set against the plan's 143 urban lines at 2050 travel (`docs/data/timeline.json`). No network model of the highway: a BPR delay curve on one segment, with and without induced traffic.

## Short answer

- **The cutover is a benefit-cost ratio of about 0.8** if a new lane kept its congestion relief for 50 years (no induced traffic): a lane-mile at $61M returns $51M. In riders that is **about 8 new daily transit trips per $M of whole-life cost** (capital plus 50 years' operating cost), roughly 13 per $M of capital. 13 of the plan's 143 urban lines ($18.8B of $231.5B, 8% of the capital) clear it.
- **With induced traffic the cutover mostly disappears.** At the fundamental law's elasticity of 1.0 (Duranton & Turner 2011) the lane fills within a decade, the time saved goes, and the extra driving's crashes and emissions outweigh what is left: ratio -0.37, below doing nothing, and every line beats it. Not charging those crashes and emissions, the decade of relief alone is worth 0.15 (127 lines clear it). At a partial elasticity of 0.6 the cutover is 0.19 (2.0 trips per $M whole-life), and 115 lines (83% of the capital) clear it.
- **The capacity argument (a line carries far more than a lane) holds only at metro headways the plan doesn't run.** At the plan's headways a metro line's capacity is about two freeway lanes, regional rail one, BRT a third; and the money buys far fewer passenger-km than a lane carries (table 2). Transit's case rests on what each new rider is worth, and on induced demand eroding what a lane is worth.
- **Verdict: still on the right track against highway widening, but not by the margin the capacity argument suggests.** For the congested metro freeways where the plan's lines run, the evidence (fundamental law; the Katy Freeway) says widening buys little lasting relief, so the plan's lines beat it. If one believes a lane keeps its relief, a typical widening (ratio ~0.8) beats most of the plan's lines (median ratio 0.34). Either way the weakest lines (ratios 0.1-0.2, table 4) lose to a widening in almost every case, and most lines are below 1 on their own: the stronger reason to trim them is that they don't return their cost, not highways.
- **Nearest and below the cutover:** Austin's, San Antonio's and a few small metros' first BRT lines and Austin's first metro line clear even the no-induced-demand case; Houston's 71 lines ($136.1B, median ratio 0.26) have none above it; regional rail (median 0.24) and later outer BRT and metro extensions are the furthest below.

Per dollar of capacity a freeway lane is in the same range as BRT and regional rail and cheaper than metro at the plan's headways. Rail's advantage is headroom: more trains cost operating money, not new right of way, and a metro line at 2-3 minute headways carries 4-5 lanes' worth.

## 2. But capacity isn't use: passenger-km a day per $M of capital

A Texas urban freeway lane carries about 25,000 vehicles a day (200,000 on 4 + 4 lanes), 27,500 passenger-km per lane-km: **724 passenger-km a day per $M** at $61M a lane-mile. The plan's lines carry their model's new daily trips (2050) x 12 km:

| Mode | Lines | Best | Median | Weakest kept | New daily trips per $M capital (median) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| BRT | 103 | 329 | 84 | 21 | 7.0 |
| Metro / light rail | 23 | 185 | 52 | 20 | 4.4 |
| Regional rail | 16 | 154 | 41 | 22 | 3.4 |
| Streetcar | 1 | 88 | 88 | 88 | 7.3 |
| All urban lines | 143 | 329 | 64 | 20 | 5.4 |

A dollar of lane carries several times the passenger-km of a dollar of the plan's lines (the median line 9% as much). But the two numbers don't mean the same thing. The line's are *new* transit trips, mostly out of cars (each worth $8 to its rider in the plan's commute logit, $12 with the wider benefits). The lane's are mostly trips that were already being made: without induced traffic they are the same drivers, a little faster; with it (Duranton & Turner: vehicle-km rise about one for one with lane-km in US metros, from more driving by residents, more trucks and in-migration, little diverted from other roads) they are new trips worth barely more than their cost to the driver, and speeds go back to where they were. Houston's Katy Freeway, widened to 18-26 lanes for $2.8B by 2008, had peak travel times 30-55% longer by 2014 than in 2011 (City Observatory, from TranStar data).

## 3. The cutover

**The widening.** A 4 + 4 lane freeway, 200,000 vehicles a day, 0.35 minutes of delay per vehicle-mile averaged over the day (468,417 person-hours a mile a year, a Texas Top 100 congested segment). One more lane each way is +25% capacity; on a BPR curve (delay ~ (volume/capacity)^4) that removes 59% of the delay. At the plan's value of time ($12/h: the commute model's 0.025 per minute over 0.125 per $, the same model that makes a new transit trip worth $8) plus 20% agglomeration, as transit gets: $1.99M a lane-mile a year, $51M over 50 years. Induced traffic (elasticity e, phased in over 10 years) refills the lane, takes back the saving, gets the rule of half for its own drivers, and adds $0.10 of crashes and emissions per vehicle-km (`texas/benefits.json`; its congestion is already in the delay curve).

In the same money, a new transit trip's $8 is 40 minutes of in-vehicle time; the widening saves each vehicle 0.21 minutes a mile, but for 200,000 vehicles a day.

**The line.** study/timing.py's ratio, unchanged: $8 a new trip to the rider, $2.32 of car-km avoided, $1.60 agglomeration, fares and operating cost, 2050 riders.

**Break-even.** A line's ratio equals the highway's H when its new daily trips per $M of whole-life cost (capital + 50 years' operating cost at 3%) = H x 10^6 / (300 x 25.7 x ($11.92 + H x $1.25 fare)).

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| No induced demand (the lane's capacity view) | 0.84 | 8.4 | 13.5 | 13 of 143 | $18.8B | 8% |
| No induced demand, cheap suburban lane (same congestion: generous) | 2.56 | 21.9 | 35.3 | 0 of 143 | $0M | 0% |
| No induced demand, urban-core rebuild | 0.34 | 3.6 | 5.8 | 70 of 143 | $103.1B | 45% |
| No induced demand, Texas's worst bottleneck (delay x2) | 1.68 | 15.5 | 25.0 | 0 of 143 | $0M | 0% |
| No induced demand, USDOT value of time for both | 1.28 | 8.9 | 14.4 | 9 of 143 | $13.7B | 6% |
| No induced demand, transit's wider benefits halved | 0.84 | 9.9 | 15.9 | 6 of 143 | $12.1B | 5% |
| Partial induced demand (elasticity 0.6) | 0.19 | 2.0 | 3.2 | 115 of 143 | $193.2B | 83% |

For scale: the plan's own selection floor is 1.0 new daily trip per $M of capital plus 20 years' operating cost (DFW Transit `metro/modelselect.py`), and a ratio of 1 (benefits equal costs) needs 9.8 per $M whole-life.

## What this leaves out

- One segment, not a network: no bottleneck shifting downstream (which lowers the highway's gain), no relief on parallel roads (which raises it), no reliability or vehicle operating savings (raises it a little).
- The highway's traffic is today's; by 2050 Texas metros grow 40-60%, which raises the delay a lane relieves and, with induced demand, how fast it refills. The lines are at 2050 travel.
- Transit's car-km benefits ($0.25 a km) assume the road space it frees stays free. The fundamental law says it refills too (Duranton & Turner find transit doesn't lower VKT), hence the last case.
- Managed (tolled or HOV) lanes, which most of the projects above are, can hold speeds with pricing and resist induced demand better than free lanes: closer to the no-induced-demand cases.
- Congestion pricing would beat both on congestion; neither the plan nor this note models it.
