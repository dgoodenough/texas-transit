Texas Transit Plan

What DFW Transit's money buys if all of Texas spends at the same rate per resident, $9.0B a year of capital to 2100: high-speed rail between the big metros, conventional trains to the neighbours and Monterrey, and rail and BRT inside every metro, with Dallas-Fort Worth running its own plan on $2.3B of that a year. Land tax near new stations helps pay for the metro lines. Move the year to watch it get built.

Intercity lines, in the order they open

Each project's ratio is travellers' gain (plus any operating surplus) over capital plus the operating deficit, across 50 years at 3%. A ratio of 1 or more means travellers' gains alone pay for it. The budget is spent either way; the ratio sets the order.

What to keep in mind

  • Benefits are travellers' gain plus wider benefits: congestion, crashes and emissions avoided for every car-km off the road and air passenger-km out of the sky, and agglomeration for urban and high-speed lines. These are allowances from US and UK practice, not modelled traffic; halve them and most urban lines fall back under a ratio of 1. Even with them, few projects pay for themselves: everything else is built on the plan's premise, spending DFW's rate, in the order that buys the most.
  • Land tax near new urban stations (a Tax Increment Reinvestment Zone on the rise in value plus a small Public Improvement District assessment, borrowed against when the line opens) pays about an eighth of the urban lines' capital, a fifth to a third in the smaller metros. It assumes cities form the districts and the uplift arrives with the line.
  • Dallas-Fort Worth runs DFW Transit's 2 million riders plan on its own timetable; what it doesn't spend, and its stations' land-tax bonds, go to the rest of the state. The statewide stations meet its network on foot: Dallas high-speed beside Union Station, Fort Worth at Fort Worth Central, Denton at its transit center.
  • An urban trip is worth about $12 in all (its rider's $8 and $4 to everyone else), an intercity trip $40-80 to its traveller. So the order still favours the Triangle's high-speed lines; the metros-first scenario shows the other way round.
  • Operating cost is outside the capital budget, as in DFW Transit. By 2100 the lines statewide, DFW included, cost about $6B a year more to run than they take in fares, and no budget pays that yet.
  • The model overrates infrequent rail: at its constant it puts about 16,000 riders a day on Austin's Red Line, and NTD counts 1,636. Regional rail and the conventional trains are likely overrated; metro and BRT are where the calibration holds.
  • For high-speed rail, the constant against car alone moves the first four corridors between 30M and 74M trips a year.
  • Values grow with Texas's population trend and nothing else: no new land use around stations, no change in driving or flying.
