Texas Transit Plan

What DFW Transit's money buys if all of Texas spends at the same rate per resident, $9.0B a year of capital to 2100: high-speed rail between the big metros, conventional trains to the neighbours and Monterrey, and rail and BRT inside every metro, with Dallas-Fort Worth running its own plan on $2.3B of that a year. Land tax near new stations helps pay for the metro lines. Move the year to watch it get built.

What it takes to build the whole map

In one statewide queue, best ratio first, the branches (Oklahoma City, Laredo, the Valley, Abilene, Lubbock and Amarillo, El Paso) come last or not at all, and all but the Valley fail a benefit-cost test on their own; the cabinet's plan (study/hsr_buildout.md) builds them anyway, as a choice to connect the state, from money of their own. Intercity lines, in the order they open

Each project's ratio is travellers' gain (plus any operating surplus) over capital plus the operating deficit, across 50 years at 3%. A ratio of 1 or more means travellers' gains alone pay for it. The budget is spent either way; the ratio sets the order.

What to keep in mind

  • Benefits are travellers' gain plus wider benefits: congestion, crashes and emissions avoided for every car-km off the road and air passenger-km out of the sky, and agglomeration for urban and high-speed lines. These are allowances from US and UK practice, not modelled traffic; halve them and most urban lines fall back under a ratio of 1. Even with them, only 21 of the 103 projects pay for themselves: everything else is built on the plan's premise, spending DFW's rate, in the order that buys the most.
  • Land tax near new urban stations (a Tax Increment Reinvestment Zone on the rise in value plus a small Public Improvement District assessment, borrowed against when the line opens) pays about 8% of the urban lines' capital, a fifth to a third in the smaller metros. It assumes cities form the districts and the uplift arrives with the line.
  • Dallas-Fort Worth runs DFW Transit's 2 million riders plan on its own timetable; what it doesn't spend, and its stations' land-tax bonds, go to the rest of the state. The statewide stations meet its network on foot: Dallas high-speed beside Union Station, Fort Worth at Fort Worth Central, Denton at its transit center, if the Oklahoma City line is built (in the best-ratio order the money runs out before it).
  • An urban trip is worth about $12 in all (its rider's $8 and $4 to everyone else), an intercity trip $40-80 to its traveller. So the order still favours the Triangle's high-speed lines; the metros-first scenario shows the other way round.
  • Operating cost is outside the capital budget, as in DFW Transit. By 2100 the lines statewide, DFW included, cost about $4.5B a year more to run than they take in fares, and no budget pays that yet.
  • The model overrates infrequent rail: at its constant it puts about 16,000 riders a day on Austin's Red Line, and NTD counts 1,636. Regional rail and the conventional trains are likely overrated; metro and BRT are where the calibration holds.
  • High-speed rail's appeal beyond its time and cost is calibrated to Brightline's Miami–Orlando riders, and falls on trips under 300 km, as on New York–Philadelphia (study/calibrate_hsr.md). Dallas–Houston rail takes about a third of trips. At the Northeast Corridor's higher appeal every high-speed ratio would be two to four times larger. Each ratio also has a P10–P90 range for cost overruns and ridership misses (study/timing.md).
  • Each metro's riders follow its own model runs in 2026, 2050 and 2100: its counties' growth trend, with DFW's 2 million plan's land use around new stations (more homes and jobs near them, a fifth of growth steered there). Those runs open every line from 2026, so late lines are credited with station areas that have had decades to grow. Intercity values grow with Texas's population trend; nothing changes in driving or flying.
